# Return on Investment (ROI) and Value on Investment (VOI)

Return on investment (ROI) na di net financial return wey digital health platform generate relative to im total cost — licensing, hardware deployment, implementation, and ongoing staffing — wey dem express as ratio or percentage. Value on investment (VOI) na broader companion measure wey capture returns wey pure financial ROI calculation no fit capture: quality-of-care improvements, patient and clinician satisfaction, health equity impact, and organizational reputation, wey none of dem convert cleanly into dollar figure but all of dem matter to whether dem suppose consider digital health investment as success. Together, dem answer two different, complementary questions: ROI ask "e pay for itself financially?"; VOI ask "e worth to do, if we account for everything wey matter?"

## Why e matter

Digital health investment wey show poor or negative ROI for im first year no automatically be failure, and one wey show strong ROI no automatically be success, if di ROI calculation ignore material value wey di programme generate outside pure cost offset — na exactly dis gap VOI dey exist to close, and organizations wey rely on ROI alone dey risk to defund programmes wey get genuine value but wey never yet, or wey go never, show up as direct financial return. For di same time, VOI more subjective and harder to benchmark than ROI, wey make am vulnerable to dem use am to justify continued investment for programme wey no actually deliver measurable value of any kind; di discipline of to report both together, with VOI components wey dem make as concrete and measurable as possible, na wetin dey keep dis framework honest instead of to become way to avoid financial accountability. Long-term digital health investment decisions — particularly for value-based care, where quality and equity outcomes dey directly tie to payment — dey increasingly require both figures side by side.

## How dem dey calculate am

```
ROI = (financial benefit − total investment cost) / total investment cost × 100

Financial benefit sources: cost per episode of care reduction, bed-day
reduction (converted to a cost figure), avoided ED visits (see ED
diversion rate), reduced readmission penalties, and any direct revenue.

Total investment cost: platform licensing, hardware/device deployment
and maintenance, implementation and integration cost, ongoing clinical
and technical staffing.

VOI is not a single formula, but a structured, multi-dimensional
scorecard combining ROI with defined, tracked non-financial value
domains, for example:
  Clinical quality  (e.g. biometric improvement rate, readmission rate)
  Patient experience (e.g. patient net promoter score, SUS score)
  Equity           (e.g. digital access rate, digital literacy rate)
  Workforce         (e.g. physician burnout rate)
Each domain is reported on its own terms, not artificially converted
to a dollar figure, alongside the financial ROI figure.
```

## Worked example

One remote monitoring programme cost $600,000 for im first year (platform licensing, device deployment, and additional clinical staffing) and generate $750,000 for quantifiable financial benefit (bed-day reduction and avoided readmission penalties). ROI na ($750,000 − $600,000) / $600,000 × 100 = 25%. Together with dis figure, di organization VOI scorecard report 5-point improvement for patient net promoter score, biometric stabilization rate of 68% for enrolled hypertension patients, and measured reduction for enrolled clinicians after-hours documentation time. None of dese three additional findings change di 25% ROI figure, but together dem build materially stronger case for continued investment than di ROI figure alone go support, particularly to stakeholders (clinical leadership, patient advocacy groups) wey weigh quality and equity outcomes as highly as financial return.

## Data sources and caveats

Di financial benefit side of ROI dey draw on di same cost and outcome data wey dem use for other places for dis book — cost per episode of care, bed-day reduction, ED diversion rate — wey dem convert into dollar figure using di organization own cost-accounting methodology, and e suppose use conservative, defensible assumptions instead of optimistic best-case ones, because ROI figure wey dem build on aggressive assumptions no go survive scrutiny from sceptical finance team. Each of VOI non-financial domains suppose use metric wey dem don already validate from somewhere else for dis book (biometric stabilization rate, patient net promoter score, digital access rate, physician burnout rate) instead of informally invented substitute, so dat di VOI scorecard itself go remain auditable and comparable over time instead of to become loosely defined narrative.

## Pitfalls

- **To report ROI alone for programme wey get material non-financial value**: dis dey risk to defund programme wey get genuine clinical, equity, or workforce value simply because dat value no show up for pure financial calculation; pair ROI with structured VOI scorecard.
- **To treat VOI as way to avoid financial accountability**: VOI suppose use concrete, previously validated metrics for each non-financial domain, no be vague or unmeasured claims of value, or e go become way to justify continued spending without genuine evidence.
- **To use optimistic or unvalidated assumptions for di ROI cost-avoidance calculation**: ROI figure wey dem build on aggressive assumptions about avoided cost no go withstand scrutiny and fit damage di credibility of future investment cases.
- **To compare ROI figures across programmes wey get very different cost structures without adjustment**: programme wey get high upfront capital cost and long payback period no suppose dem compare am on single-year ROI figure against one wey get low upfront cost and fast payback without to account for di different time horizons wey dey involved.
